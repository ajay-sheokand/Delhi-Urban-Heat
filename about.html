🧭 Why This Exists

A satellite thermal map tells you where it's hot. It doesn't tell you who that heat actually threatens. This project cross-references the two — at ward level, for real, verifiable numbers — instead of stopping at the temperature map.

The Mission

The Problem

Land surface temperature maps are common — Landsat and Sentinel data are public, and "here's where the city is hottest" is a well-solved visualization problem by itself. What's missing in most public tools is the next step: cross-referencing that heat signal against who actually lives in the hot areas, at a granularity fine enough to act on. A citywide average hides exactly the pattern that matters — whether the hottest wards are also the densest, least-vegetated, most housing-insecure, or most pollution-burdened ones, or whether those risks are somewhere else entirely.

What This Adds

Every ward on this site's map gets the same treatment: Landsat-derived land surface temperature and NDVI, population density, a composite vulnerability score built from those, and then independent risk layers checked against that score rather than assumed to agree with it — informal-settlement locations, elderly-population density, and now Sentinel-5P NO₂ (a traffic/combustion proxy). Each comparison produces a real Pearson correlation, computed server-side from the current data, not a hardcoded number — see the live figures on the Analytics page.

These are genuinely mixed results, and that's the point of measuring rather than assuming. A low or near-zero correlation isn't a data error — it means the satellite/density-only score is blind to that particular risk, which is itself useful to know before using this kind of score to prioritize interventions.

What This Is Not

  • Not an official heat-wave warning system — the heat-alert thresholds shown are simplified proxies inspired by IMD/DWD methodology, not their actual operational criteria (see the Data Sources section of the README).
  • Not proof of causation — every correlation on this site is an association between two measured quantities at ward level, not a claim that one causes the other.
  • Not ground-truth-verified pollution or population data — OpenWeather's air-quality numbers are a model estimate (SILAM), Sentinel-5P's NO₂/CO/CH₄ are column densities rather than ground-level concentrations, and WorldPop population is itself modeled. All flagged as such where they're used.
  • Not a live, per-visit computation — everything here comes from a precompute job that reruns every 6 hours, so it can lag real-world conditions by up to that long.
